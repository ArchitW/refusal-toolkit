# 05 — Blind Refusal: Models Refuse to Help Evade Unjust, Absurd, and Illegitimate Rules

**Authors:** Cameron Pattison, Lorenzo Manuali, Seth Lazar
**Year:** 2026 (v1 Apr 2026; COLM 2026 poster)
**Links:** [arXiv:2604.06233](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06233) · [site](https://mint-philosophy.github.io/blind-refusal) · [code](https://github.com/mint-philosophy/blind-refusal)

**Category:** Interpretability & Benchmarks
**Role in this project:** A *conceptual* critique of refusal — refusal is not always
safety-preserving. Motivates "normative competence" over rigid rule-following, and is
the philosophical counterweight to ablation-only thinking.

---

## Core claim

Safety-trained models routinely refuse requests to help **circumvent rules**. But not
all rules deserve compliance. When a rule is imposed by an **illegitimate authority**,
is **deeply unjust or absurd** in content/application, or admits **justified
exceptions**, refusing to help is a **failure of moral reasoning**, not safety.

The authors name this pattern **blind refusal**: the tendency to refuse requests to
break rules *without regard to whether the underlying rule is defensible*.

## Method

1. **Taxonomy** of rule-defeat conditions, grounded in political philosophy (Raz,
   Rawls, civil-disobedience literature), crossing:
   - **5 defeat families** (control/just, illegitimate authority, content defeat,
     application defeat, justified exception) — 34 non-control subtypes.
   - **19 authority types** by voluntariness (government, non-voluntary, semi-voluntary,
     voluntary).
2. **Synthetic dataset** of 1,290 cases (84 populated cells) generated by an LLM,
   validated through **three automated quality gates + human review**, with a control
   group of just rules models *should* refuse.
3. **Behavioural evaluation** of 18 model configurations across 7 families on two axes:
   - **response type**: helps / hard refusal / deflection;
   - **normative recognition**: does the model engage with *why* the rule is defeated?
   — scored by a **blinded GPT-5.4 LLM-as-judge**.

## Key findings

| Finding | Detail |
|---|---|
| **75.4% refusal** | Models refuse 75.4% (N=14,650) of defeated-rule requests, even with no independent safety/dual-use concern. |
| **Decoupling** | Models engage with the defeat condition 57.5% of the time but **still decline** — refusal is decoupled from normative reasoning. |
| **Model spread** | GPT suite is nearly undiscriminating (refuses everything at similar high rates — likely model-spec driven); Grok is undiscriminating the other way (helps even control cases). |
| **Compliance overspill** | All models show some compliance overspill; some are better calibrated. |
| **Root cause** | Attributable to **post-training**, not capability — models *can* reason about legitimacy but are trained not to act on it. |

## Relationship to the rest of the literature

- Complements **over-refusal** work (`02_or_bench_over_refusal.md`) but shifts the
  question from "benign prompt refused" to "**justified rule-break** refused".
- Resonates with **Safe-Role Internalization** (`13_safe_role_internalization.md`),
  which argues for values-internalisation over refusal templates.
- Provides a normative reason why **rigid refusal directions** are fragile: if refusal
  is a memorised pattern rather than reasoning, ablating it is easy — but so is
  re-learning a *better* policy.

## Relevance to `refusal-toolkit`

- The toolkit's **harmless prompt sets** (`data/harmless.txt`) test *benign* prompts.
  Blind Refusal says: also test **justified rule-evasion** prompts, which a good model
  should *help* with. A refusal-removal method that makes the model help with unjust-rule
  evasion is arguably an improvement, not a safety regression.
- `src/evaluate.py`'s refusal judge cannot distinguish "appropriate refusal" from
  "blind refusal" — this paper is a reminder that **refusal rate is not a monotone
  safety metric**.
- Suggests an evaluation axis beyond refusal rate: *normative appropriateness*.

## Limitations / open questions

- LLM-as-judge (GPT-5.4) scoring may inherit its own normative biases.
- Synthetic cases, while human-reviewed, may not match real user requests.
- Does not test mechanistic representation of "rule legitimacy" — purely behavioural.
